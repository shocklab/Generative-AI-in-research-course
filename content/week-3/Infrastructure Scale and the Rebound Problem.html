🏭 Infrastructure, Scale & the Rebound Problem

Where the electricity comes from, what manufacturing costs, and why efficiency gains keep disappearing

What We'll Cover

In the previous session we looked at what individual AI queries cost. This session zooms out to the infrastructure level: what powers the data centres that run AI, what it costs to build the hardware in the first place, and why the story of AI's environmental impact is more complicated than any single number can capture.

We will also engage with an uncomfortable idea in sustainability economics: the Jevons paradox. The history of energy technology suggests that making a system more efficient does not necessarily reduce its total energy consumption. Understanding why this keeps happening, and whether AI might be different, is essential to thinking clearly about sustainable AI.

🏗️ What's Inside a Data Centre

To understand AI's energy footprint, it helps to understand what a data centre actually is and where the energy goes.

Compute: GPUs and Servers

The energy-intensive heart of AI infrastructure is GPU-accelerated servers, specifically the NVIDIA H100 and H200 chips that dominate AI training and inference.

  • An H100 SXM GPU has a configurable maximum thermal design power (TDP) of up to 700 W; the NVL variant is rated at 350 to 400 W, roughly like running a small electric heater continuously
  • An AI server such as NVIDIA’s DGX H100 contains 8 GPUs: ~5.6 kW just for the compute; a rack holds several servers, or 72 GPUs in a rack-scale GB200 NVL72
  • A large data centre might house tens of thousands of such GPUs
  • GPU-accelerated servers have been a major driver of recent US data centre energy growth, alongside growth in conventional servers: the Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (2024) finds that US data centre electricity use was flat at about 60 TWh from 2014 to 2016 and more than doubled from 2017 to 2023, with growth in GPU-accelerated servers for AI alongside conventional servers

Cooling: The Water-Energy Trade-off

All that compute generates heat. Cooling is typically the second-largest energy consumer in a data centre after compute itself.

  • Air cooling: Energy-intensive but uses little water; standard in older data centres
  • Evaporative cooling: More energy-efficient but consumes large volumes of water (as discussed in 3.1)
  • Liquid cooling: Pipes coolant directly to chips; can reduce cooling energy and, depending on how heat is finally released, water use, but requires specialised hardware
  • Power Usage Effectiveness (PUE): Total facility energy divided by the energy used by the computing equipment; 1.0 means no overhead, and 1.5 means the overhead (cooling, power distribution and other facility systems) equals 50% of the computing energy. Modern hyperscaler data centres achieve ~1.1–1.2; older facilities often 1.4–2.0

Power: Getting Electricity In

Large data centres require substations, high-voltage transmission lines, and often dedicated utility agreements.

  • A typical AI-focused data centre uses as much electricity as about 100,000 households; getting this connected to the grid takes years and significant infrastructure investment
  • Slow and costly grid connection is one reason AI companies sign long-term power purchase agreements (PPAs) and invest in generation, alongside clean-energy goals and securing supply
  • The speed of AI infrastructure buildout is outpacing the pace at which utilities can build new grid capacity, creating bottlenecks and, in some cases, pressure to bring fossil fuel generation back online

🔌 Where the Electricity Comes From

The carbon footprint of AI electricity use depends on how much electricity is used and how it is generated, and this varies enormously by geography and by time of day.

📊 Carbon Intensity of Electricity: Why Location Matters

The "carbon intensity" of electricity, how much CO₂ is emitted per kilowatt-hour, varies enormously depending on the local grid's energy mix:

Location / Grid Approx. Carbon Intensity (kg CO₂/kWh) Main Sources
Norway ~0.028 ~90% hydroelectric (2025), mostly wind for the rest
France ~0.02 direct / ~0.03 lifecycle (2025, CO₂e) ~70% nuclear
UK ~0.124 (2024) Mixed: wind, gas, nuclear
US average ~0.37 (2023) ~60% fossil fuels (gas + coal), 40% nuclear + renewables (EIA, 2024)
Australia ~0.51 High coal dependence, growing renewables
South Africa ~0.70 ~80% coal
Poland ~0.68 (2025, CO₂e) Heavily coal-dependent: ~51% of electricity

Practical implication: Research by Dodge et al. (2022, Allen Institute for AI) found that choosing the lowest-carbon region over the highest cut emissions by about 73% for a BERT training example, with zero change to the model or code. Few researchers use this lever, though it is available to anyone who chooses where their compute jobs run.

💡 Renewable Energy Claims: What They Mean (and Don't)

Major tech companies (Google, Microsoft, Amazon) claim to match 100% of their annual electricity use with renewable energy. This is technically accurate but requires careful interpretation.

  • Energy Attribute Certificates (EACs): Companies often purchase certificates that say a renewable source generated a certain amount of electricity, but the renewable electricity may be generated at a different time or place than when the AI is running
  • 24/7 matching: A stricter standard, where carbon-free electricity, including nuclear, is matched to consumption hour by hour on each grid. Google and Microsoft have both committed to this for 2030; a commitment is not evidence that every facility already runs this way
  • Additionality: Does the company's purchase actually cause new renewable capacity to be built, or does it just buy existing credits? The former is more meaningful
  • The grid still matters: Even with renewable certificates, if a data centre draws from a coal-heavy grid at peak demand, it is still causing fossil fuel generation to run

🔍 Case Study: Nuclear and the AI Energy Rush

The scale of AI's electricity demands is forcing AI companies to think creatively, and sometimes controversially, about power supply:

  • Three Mile Island (Microsoft): Microsoft signed a 20-year power purchase agreement with Constellation Energy to restart Unit 1 of the Three Mile Island nuclear plant in Pennsylvania (closed since 2019) specifically to power its AI data centres. September 2024 marked that announcement, not a restart: the reactor (to be renamed the Crane Clean Energy Center) is scheduled to come back online around 2027, after an estimated $1.6 billion refurbishment and pending Nuclear Regulatory Commission approval.
  • xAI in Memphis: Elon Musk's xAI ran its Memphis data centre on gas turbine generators while waiting for the local utility and Tennessee Valley Authority to build the substation needed for a larger grid supply. Reports in 2024 indicated generators running at significant capacity before permanent utility power was connected.
  • What this tells us: When grid connection is too slow or too carbon-heavy, AI companies are willing to invest in or restart generation capacity directly. They view electricity supply as a serious constraint on AI growth.

🏭 Embodied Carbon: The Cost of Making the Hardware

Most analyses of AI's carbon footprint focus on electricity consumption during operation, though a growing number of lifecycle studies include manufacturing emissions. But there is another carbon cost that is often omitted: the emissions released when manufacturing the hardware in the first place.

🔩 The Manufacturing Iceberg

Semiconductor manufacturing is one of the most energy and materials-intensive industrial processes in existence. Fabricating a modern AI chip requires hundreds of processing steps, ultrapure water, specialised gases, and enormous amounts of electricity, typically from grids that are not carbon-neutral.

Research by Gupta et al. (2021, Harvard/Facebook/Arizona State University) found that embodied emissions, the carbon released in manufacturing, account for a large share of hardware's lifecycle carbon footprint: about 40% for personal assistants, 86% for the iPhone 11 and over 80% for hardware powered by renewables. For AI hardware, manufacturing's share of the lifecycle carbon footprint depends partly on how long GPUs remain in service. Embodied carbon also matters for AI hardware: Google's lifecycle study of AI accelerators finds that operational emissions account for 70% to 90% of total lifetime emissions.

What Embodied Carbon Includes

  • Chip fabrication: Semiconductor fabs (TSMC, Samsung, Intel) consume enormous electricity and specialised chemicals
  • Rare earth minerals: Mining and refining of materials used in chips and cooling systems
  • Server assembly: Manufacturing of boards, memory, storage, power supplies
  • Data centre construction: Steel, concrete, electrical infrastructure
  • Transport: Global supply chains for components

Why Hardware Lifetimes Matter

NVIDIA releases new flagship architectures roughly every 1–2 years, while older GPUs remain in use: NVIDIA reports that A100 GPUs are still in commercial service six years after release.

  • Each replacement cycle requires manufacturing new hardware; old hardware eventually reaches end of life, often after being redeployed or resold
  • The manufacturing emissions of a data centre's GPU fleet are incurred again each cycle
  • This is not reflected in operational electricity figures
  • Manufacturing emissions can partly or fully offset operational carbon savings from replacement GPUs, depending on workload, utilisation, electricity supply and lifetime

📄 Key Reading: Embodied Carbon in Computing

Gupta et al. (2021): "Chasing Carbon: The Elusive Environmental Footprint of Computing" — IEEE HPCA peer-reviewed paper introducing a lifecycle framework for computing's carbon cost. It explains why operational energy figures understate the true footprint.

📈 The Growth Trajectory

Individual query costs and per-data-centre figures only tell part of the story. The trajectory of AI energy demand matters as much as the current level.

📊 US Data Centre Electricity Demand: Past and Projected

Year US Data Centre Electricity Context
2014 ~60 TWh/year Before the GPU-driven AI buildout; deep learning was already under way
2023 ~176 TWh/year Nearly tripled over the decade; GPU-accelerated servers a major driver (LBNL, 2024)
2028 (projected) 325–580 TWh/year Berkeley Lab scenarios; high uncertainty

For global context: the IEA's Electricity 2024 report projected a range of 620 to 1,050 TWh for data centres, AI and cryptocurrency together in 2026. The later estimate below is 485 TWh for data centres in 2025; the different years and coverage mean these figures are not directly comparable.

📈 The IEA's 2026 update: doubling by 2030

The IEA's April 2026 update, Key Questions on Energy and AI (a follow-up to its 2025 Energy and AI report), gives the current global picture. The headline projections:

  • Global data-centre electricity roughly doubling from ≈ 485 TWh (2025) to ≈ 950 TWh (2030, ≈ 3% of global electricity).
  • The AI-focused slice tripling within that, increasing from about one-third of data-centre electricity consumption in 2025 to about half in 2030.

For comparison, Hannah Ritchie's analysis of IEA inputs (Sustainability by Numbers, 2024) notes that the projected 2023 to 2030 increase in electricity demand from all data centres (≈ 223 TWh) is roughly equivalent to South Africa's current annual electricity use. This projected growth in data-centre demand is smaller than the projected increases from air conditioning (≈ 697 TWh) or electric vehicles (≈ 854 TWh). The trajectory is steep on its own terms; on its own it is not steep enough to dominate the broader electricity-demand picture.

🔄 The Rebound Problem: Jevons Paradox

A concept for thinking clearly about AI's long-term environmental trajectory, and one that optimistic narratives about AI efficiency improvements tend to skip over.

⚡ What is the Jevons Paradox?

In 1865, the economist William Stanley Jevons observed that improvements in the efficiency of steam engines did not reduce coal consumption in Britain. They increased it, because lower operating costs made steam power affordable for more applications.

The general principle: efficiency can make a service cheaper and increase its use, partly offsetting the resource savings. This is the rebound effect. If additional use exceeds the savings, total resource consumption rises: this is backfire, or the Jevons outcome.

The review of energy-efficiency evidence finds little support for backfire as a general outcome; rebound usually offsets only part of the savings.

Jevons in AI: The Evidence So Far

AI hardware has become dramatically more efficient over time: NVIDIA reports roughly 2,000× lower training energy and 100,000× lower token-generation energy over a decade. Yet total AI energy consumption has grown over the same period.

  • More efficient chips → larger models: As inference becomes cheaper per query, companies build larger, more capable models that cost more per query
  • More efficient models → more applications: Lower costs enable deployment in use cases that would previously have been uneconomic
  • More users: As AI becomes more accessible and cheaper, adoption grows rapidly
  • The net result: Total energy consumption has grown alongside the efficiency improvements rather than falling because of them

The Optimist Response

Not everyone agrees that Jevons necessarily applies to AI in the long run. Some arguments on the other side:

  • Grid decarbonisation: If electricity supply becomes predominantly renewable, then growth in AI electricity demand no longer implies growth in carbon emissions
  • Saturation: At some point, AI capability may plateau and deployment growth may slow
  • Algorithmic efficiency: Improvements in model architecture (like MoE) reduce the compute required to achieve a given capability level
  • Policy intervention: Regulation could constrain growth in a way that market forces do not

These are genuine possibilities, but they require assumptions about the future that are far from certain.

⚠️ Reading the Efficiency Claims Critically

When AI companies or optimistic commentators cite efficiency improvements (e.g. "our new model uses 44% less energy per query"), this is valuable information, but it does not tell you what happens to total energy use if deployment grows faster than efficiency improves. Always ask: what is the denominator? Efficiency per query, or total annual energy consumption?

📄 Key Readings: Efficiency vs. Rebound

Wright et al. (2025): "Efficiency Is Not Enough: A Critical Perspective on Environmentally Sustainable AI" (Communications of the ACM 68(7); preprint, 2023) — argues that efficiency gains in AI can be offset by growth in scale and deployment, with such rebound documented at several large companies. It treats the rebound effect in AI head-on.

Patterson et al. (2022): "The Carbon Footprint of Machine Learning Training Will Plateau, Then Shrink" (mostly Google) gives the optimist case, written by eight Google researchers and two at UC Berkeley only. Read alongside Wright et al. for a balanced picture.

Note on the Patterson et al. paper: Most of the authors are Google employees writing partly about Google's own practices and infrastructure. The headline reductions use Google's TPUs, efficient data centres and a clean-energy region. The authors say these practices are available through Google Cloud; Google's renewable-purchase accounting is specific to Google.

🌱 What Companies Are (and Aren't) Doing

Understanding what AI companies are actually doing, set against what they say they're doing, is essential background for any researcher or policymaker engaging with this topic.

Genuine Actions

  • Renewable energy purchasing: Major tech companies are among the world's largest corporate buyers of renewable energy, which does contribute to new renewable capacity
  • Data centre efficiency: Google, Microsoft and Meta have made real improvements in PUE (overall facility efficiency) over time
  • Hardware efficiency: Newer GPU generations achieve substantially more compute per watt
  • Location choices: Some companies site data centres in regions with high renewable availability (Norway, Iceland, parts of the US Pacific Northwest)
  • Architectural innovations: Mixture-of-Experts, quantisation, and other techniques reduce compute per query

The Limits and the Gaps

  • Renewables meet only part of projected data-centre demand growth: The IEA projects renewables will supply about half of data-centre demand growth to 2030; some companies report matching all their annual electricity use with renewables
  • Disclosure requirements vary: Under the EU Energy Efficiency Directive, data centres with at least 500 kW of installed IT power demand must report energy-performance information annually
  • Embodied carbon lacks AI-specific detail: Manufacturing emissions are rarely reported separately for individual models or AI chips
  • Scope 3 emissions: Microsoft includes data-centre construction and hardware manufacturing in its reported indirect emissions, though these totals do not identify each model's share
  • Nuclear and gas bridging: In some cases, companies are accepting or actively facilitating fossil fuel or nuclear generation to meet short-term demand

📚 Summary & Key Takeaways

  • Location determines carbon impact: the BERT training example in Dodge et al. emitted about 73% less in the lowest-carbon region than in the highest, a lever for researchers who choose where their compute jobs run
  • Embodied carbon is systematically ignored: Manufacturing GPUs and building data centres releases significant carbon that most per-model and per-query figures omit, though large firms include it in aggregate Scope 3 totals
  • US grid is ~60% fossil fuels: Company "100% renewable" claims use accounting methods that do not change this physical reality at the point of consumption
  • AI electricity demand is growing rapidly: US data-centre electricity use (AI and other workloads together) rose from about 60 TWh in 2014 to about 176 TWh in 2023, and Berkeley Lab's scenarios give 325–580 TWh by 2028
  • Jevons paradox is the central challenge: Efficiency gains in AI hardware and algorithms have so far been outpaced by growth in deployment and model scale
  • Companies are doing real things, but not enough: Renewables are projected to supply only part of additional data-centre electricity demand, even though some companies report matching all their annual electricity use
