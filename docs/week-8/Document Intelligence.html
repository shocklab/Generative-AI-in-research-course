Overview

Research lives in PDFs. Supplementary data tables, scanned archival documents, preprints, journal papers, grant reports: the information researchers need most is often locked in formats that resist easy extraction. AI has made substantial progress here, but the gap between “I can read this PDF” and “I can reliably extract its data” is wider than most tools acknowledge.

This sub-lesson covers the technical reasons PDFs are hard, maps the current tool landscape with accuracy figures, looks at why complex tables remain unsolved, and gives you a practical workflow for document AI in research settings.

Why PDFs Are Harder Than They Look

The Technical Reality of PDF Structure

PDFs were designed for printing. They store page text as positioned glyphs; tagged PDFs can also store logical structure, but tagging is optional and many files lack it. In a typical untagged PDF, a column of numbers is not stored as a table: it is stored as individual text fragments at specific page coordinates. Some PDF tools reconstruct logical structure (paragraphs, headings, table cells, figure captions) from positioned text. Multimodal models can also read rendered pages directly using vision, while tagged PDFs can supply explicit structure.

There are three distinct PDF types, each with different extraction characteristics:

  • Native PDF (text layer): text is stored as text; extraction is relatively reliable. Most modern journal PDFs fall into this category.
  • Image PDF (scanned): the entire page is an image; extraction needs OCR to recover text, though layout analysis can run before OCR. Older archival documents and some supplementary files are often image PDFs.
  • Mixed PDF: some pages have text layers, some are scanned. Mixed PDFs are common in theses and archives and are the most error-prone type. A thesis that includes a scanned signature page is technically a mixed PDF.

Identify your PDF type before choosing a tool. That habit saves more time than any other in document extraction work.

Current Best Practice Recommendation

For most research use cases: Docling handles standard academic PDFs well; its team's benchmark found it fastest locally on CPU, while MinerU was faster on an Nvidia L4 GPU. For tables in supplementary materials, LlamaParse is a widely used cloud option but needs a LlamaCloud account, used through an API key or its web interface. For scanned documents, Marker runs full OCR on every page. One practical approach is a hybrid: Docling for speed, and a second parser such as LlamaParse for the tables that matter most, checked by hand. OmniDocBench (CVPR 2025) evaluates Docling but not LlamaParse, so compare both on your own documents (version 1.6, released in April 2026, added 296 harder pages; the top overall score on its leaderboard is about 97 out of 100).

The Complex Table Problem

The Central Unresolved Challenge

A complex table is any table with merged cells, nested headers, multi-level row or column spans, footnotes referenced within cells, or values that span multiple rows. These are extremely common in research: ANOVA tables, regression output tables, pharmaceutical trial results, census data extracts. If you work with quantitative data, you will often work with complex tables.

Current performance: even the best tools (LlamaParse, Docling) break down on complex tables. The specific failure modes are well-documented:

  • Row merging: cells that span multiple rows are often split into duplicate entries or lost entirely
  • Multi-level headers: a header spanning three columns can become three disconnected headers, depending on the parser and output format with no parent-child relationship preserved
  • Footnote associations: the superscript “a” in a cell that refers to footnote “a” at the bottom of the table is lost; the association between cell and note is not reconstructed
  • Reading order: in some table layouts, the logical reading order (how a human reads the data) differs from the spatial order (how glyphs are positioned on the page); tools that follow spatial order produce structurally invalid output

Practical consequence: always verify extracted table data cell by cell for any table with merged cells or complex structure. Do not assume extraction accuracy for complex tables regardless of which tool you use.

The “Lost in the Middle” Problem

A Long-Document Limitation

When you give an AI model a long document, it has historically paid more attention to content at the beginning and end than to content in the middle. This was documented systematically by Liu et al. (2024) in “Lost in the Middle: How Language Models Use Long Contexts” (Transactions of the Association for Computational Linguistics, arXiv:2307.03172), a finding whose relevance depends on the model and task. A 2026 evaluation found perfect single-fact retrieval for its strongest models at the three tested depths (25%, 50% and 75% of the context), while positional weaknesses persisted for weaker models and harder tasks.

For researchers: if you are asking an AI to find specific information in a long paper (methodological details, a contradictory finding, a specific numerical result), do not assume that “1M-token context window” means equal attention to all 1M tokens. For important information, extract the relevant section and query it separately rather than relying on the AI to correctly locate it within the full document. This is the safer workflow even where the underlying limitation has improved.

Scanned and Handwritten Documents

Printed, Legible Handwriting

In tests of 2024 models, GPT-4o and Claude 3.5 Sonnet achieved roughly 3–4% word error rate on the IAM and RIMES handwritten text benchmarks; GPT-4o reports ~1.69% character error rate on RIMES (Crosilla et al., 2025, Journal of Documentation 81(7), DOI:10.1108/JD-03-2025-0082; preprint arXiv:2503.15195). Specialised open models like Allen AI's olmOCR-2-7B (Oct 2025, arXiv:2510.19817) match or exceed the included commercial systems on the authors' English-language document OCR benchmark, though they are not primarily evaluated on handwriting. Accuracy falls on historical handwriting, and further outside English: in the same benchmark the best character error rate was about 7% on English historical papers (Bentham), above 20% on two Italian historical collections and about 41% on the German READ2016 set.

Archival Documents

In GlotOCR Bench's synthetic image degradation test, accuracy fell for all six models tested, with the size of the drop varying by model and script. Budget time for manual review on archival material. AI can accelerate the work but cannot replace careful human checking for documents where accuracy matters.

Multi-Language OCR

Most tools perform well on printed English and Western European languages. Performance is measurably lower for many scripts underrepresented in training data, including several African and Asian scripts, though Japanese can perform as well as Latin. For research that involves documents in Zulu, Xhosa, Afrikaans, or other African languages, verify tool support explicitly before committing to a workflow.

Practical Tool: Marker

Marker OCRs scanned pages; bad embedded text triggers full-page OCR in balanced mode and may receive block-level repair in fast mode, and its force_ocr option OCRs every page, which lets it handle scanned documents at some cost in speed. For archival research where documents may lack a text layer, test recognition accuracy on a representative sample; Marker's documentation directs full-page OCR tasks to tools such as Chandra or surya.

Document AI in Research Workflows

The following workflow reflects best practices for integrating document AI into academic research, with accuracy verification at each stage where errors are most likely to occur.

  1. Identify your document type first (native PDF, scanned, or mixed): document type helps guide tool choice and expected accuracy; scan quality, resolution, contrast, rotation and text characteristics also matter. Open the PDF, zoom in, and try selecting text: if text is selectable, it has a text layer; if not, it is usually a scanned image.
  2. For native PDFs with standard structure: Docling works well and is free, local, and produces clean Markdown output. Install via pip install docling; the Python API is straightforward for batch processing.
  3. For complex tables in supplementary files: use LlamaParse and verify every extracted table against the visual layout. LlamaParse’s premium mode combined multimodal models with rule-based text parsing in the deprecated Parse v1 API. LlamaIndex directs new integrations to Parse v2; verify extracted tables against the original document.
  4. For scanned documents: Marker or Azure Document Intelligence; budget time for manual checking of all tables and numerical values.
  5. For long documents with specific target information: extract the relevant section (the methods section, a specific table, a particular results subsection) before querying rather than querying the full document. This directly mitigates the “lost in the middle” effect.
  6. Always check extracted tables against the visual layout: open the PDF alongside the extracted output and compare. For complex tables, check every cell. For simple tables, spot-check at least the header row and first and last data rows.
  7. For batch processing multiple papers: start with a sample of 5–10 where you know the correct values, measure accuracy before scaling to the full corpus. Discovering a systematic error after processing 200 papers is far more costly than discovering it after 10.

Sub-Lesson 3 Summary

PDF structure: PDFs store text as positioned glyphs, not structured content. The three types (native, scanned, and mixed) need different processing or settings, including OCR for scans, and yield different accuracy levels, though one package can handle both parsing and OCR.

Tool landscape: Docling (best local option for standard academic PDFs), LlamaParse (cloud, widely used for tables), Marker (scans require an OCR-capable configuration), Unstructured (versatile). A hybrid approach is one practical option to test on your own documents.

Complex tables: The central unsolved problem. Row merging, multi-level headers, footnote associations and reading order are known failure points even for the best tools. Always verify complex table extractions cell by cell.

Lost in the middle: AI models often use information in the middle of long documents less well than information at the start or end. Extract specific sections before querying rather than querying full documents.
